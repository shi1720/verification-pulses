External verification can repair individual records without moving a self-reinforcing population out of an incorrect basin. We study truth-directed reset pulses in an asynchronous nonlinear consensus process. A minimum-fuel theorem gives the basin-crossing cost under a peak verification constraint. For finite populations, a birth-death scale function composed with a counted reset pulse gives an exact subsequent wrong-consensus probability. For $N$ slots, initial error fraction $x_0$, and basin boundary $b$, the critical budget is $N\log(x_0/b)$ checks plus an order-$\sqrt{N}$ correction. Its variance separates repeated-target occupancy from autonomous amplification after checking stops. In a 16-slot majority system with 14 initially wrong slots, the mean-field threshold is nine checks, but 23 are required for 95 percent eventual recovery. We show that identical mean-field drifts can have different recovery margins, and distinguish random addressing from distinct-target checking and evidence broadcast. A prospectively specified study of two pinned language models, including 108 held-out trajectories, tests local response regimes and equal-budget schedules against independently calibrated finite-state predictions. The empirical audit also shows that a pooled response curve can predict the mean while severely underestimating terminal consensus occupancy.
